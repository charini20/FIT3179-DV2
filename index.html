Australia’s Native Mammals

Explore Australia’s remarkable mammals and their uncertain future.

WHAT THE NUMBERS TELL US

A Diverse Fauna Facing Growing Pressures

Australia is home to remarkable native mammals. Many live nowhere else on Earth.

But some have already disappeared. Of the 152 mammal entries on Australia’s national conservation list, 39 are classified as Extinct — about one in four.

The other 113 entries are classified as Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered.

Together, these figures show that extinction is only part of the story: most listed mammals remain present, but many are still under serious pressure.

25.7% of the 152 listed entries are classified as Extinct.

UNDERSTANDING THE FAMILIES

Family patterns at a glance

Five families account for most listings

Five of the 29 mammal families account for 110 of 152 listed entries. Rodents (Muridae, 33) lead, followed by kangaroos and wallabies (Macropodidae, 28).

72.4% of all listed entries fall within these five families.

Conservation patterns differ by family

The conservation mix varies. Quolls and dunnarts (Dasyuridae) have no extinct entries here, while bandicoots (Peramelidae) have the highest extinct share.

50% of the bandicoot family’s listed entries are Extinct.

FROM PLACES TO PRESSURE

Where listings are concentrated — and how severe they are

Queensland and Western Australia have the most state-linked threatened mammal listings, followed by New South Wales.

These are absolute listing counts, not extinction-risk rates or animal populations. Larger states can contain more habitats and listed species; a mammal may also appear in multiple states.

49threatened mammal listings are associated with Queensland — the highest state count.

Victoria has a high share of Endangered or Critically Endangered listings (57.1%) compared with most states.

Western Australia has the largest Vulnerable share (62.5%). The ACT has a slightly higher severe share, but from only 12 listings.

A DIFFERENT VIEW OF EXTINCTION

Which states have the highest extinct share?

South Australia has the highest share of entries classified as Extinct — 34.4% of its state-linked listings.

Western Australia follows at 29.4%, while Tasmania has a much lower share at 8.3%.

The map uses percentages, not animal populations. Mammal entries can be associated with more than one state.

34.4%of South Australia’s state-linked listings are Extinct — 21 of 61 entries.
CHANGING OVER TIME

When did Australia's threatened mammals decline most?

The biggest drop was from 2000 to 2005: the mammal index fell by 32.8 points in just five years.

There were rises as well, including +9.9 points from 2020 to 2022. But these did not reverse the long-term decline.

Across monitored threatened and near-threatened native mammals, the index ended 38% below its 1985 baseline. It does not describe every Australian mammal species.

BEHIND THE DECLINE

What's threatening Australia's native mammals?

Feral cats stand out: they were identified as a risk factor for 70.3% of the 138 assessed mammal species.

Changed fire regimes (45.7%) and red foxes (42.0%) follow, highlighting the combined pressure of altered habitats and introduced predators.

Habitat loss and fragmentation (32.6%) and livestock or feral herbivores (21.7%) add further risks, even for species facing predation.

The study assessed threats facing 138 native mammal species, highlighting the need for conservation strategies that address several environmental pressures.

PROTECTION IN PRACTICE

Are measures being taken to protect native mammals?

The 2024 assessment records 98 cat-free islands and 26 fenced havens protecting native mammals from cats.

Each dot marks a refuge, not an animal count. Zoom into the Kimberley (30 sites) and Pilbara (27 sites) to separate closely spaced island refuges.

These are the seven most-recorded threatened mammals found in both refuge types.

Fenced havens more often record woylies (46%) and bilbies (42%). Golden bandicoots occur at similar shares in both.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE SHOWS

Is conservation linked to better outcomes for native mammals?

Medium-sized mammals show the clearest contrast: +40% at managed sites versus −55% where no management was known.

Large mammals follow a similar pattern (+48% with management, −61% without), while small mammals declined in both groups (−96% and −98%).

These results suggest conservation management may be linked to better outcomes for medium and large mammals, while highlighting the need for targeted protection across different size groups.
